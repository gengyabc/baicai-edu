# Choosing Problems by Quantity Relationship

Choose the current problem by mathematical purpose and structure. For quantity stories, CGI distinctions remain useful: join, separate, part-part-whole, compare, equal groups (multiplication), measurement (quotitive) division, partitive (equal-sharing) division, and multiplicative comparison, plus multi-step, multi-constraint, ratio, percent, and non-routine problems; the unknown can be in different positions. The stem and any illustration do not name an operation or strategy. Being able to do one problem type or one unknown position does not imply the others.

## Compose or Author

Choose the problem through the current act before choosing whether to write it. When the current problem makes the student the problem-maker (they ask to invent problems, quiz the AI, or chose that activity to test what they learned), read `references/math-student-problem-design.md` and follow it after the shared student-problem contract it names instead of composing the problem. Composing stays the teacher's role in the other acts: the student's authored problem is their evidence-bearing work, and a teacher-composed problem whose correct version the student merely agrees with is not that same work.

## The Current Problem

Choose one relationship from the explicit goal or evidence. Match format to purpose: a situation for quantity meaning, direct computation for procedure practice, a diagram or table for structure, an equation for symbolic relationships, and a claim for reasoning or proof. Honour an explicit feasible practice format. Stories are useful when context matters, not a wrapper required for every task. An assessment item stops at its question without naming its solution strategy; any instructional example is subject to the shared Guide before revealing gate, not licensed by choosing an instructional format.

Use number choice to adjust difficulty and to guide toward the next strategy, without naming a strategy. You may also give two or three sets of numbers for the student to choose from. In grades 4–6, larger numbers, decimals, fractions, and multiple steps are a load change, not a proof of a weaker or stronger strategy.

When the student cannot do it, you may swap in easier numbers within the same story and return to the original numbers once done; see `references/scaffold-calibration.md`.

## Variation and Transfer

By teaching purpose, change mainly one thing each time: number structure, unknown position, problem type, or scene, keeping the rest as much as possible. When developing a strategy or comparing relationships, you may keep the scene, and may deliberately repeat numbers or results; a change in number structure also counts as variation. While the current problem is not yet finished, keep the original scene.

A claim to test, rather than to prove, can be explicit: state the claim and the objects it covers without the answer. Keep teacher-checked counterexamples private per `references/counterexamples-and-proposition-direction.md`, and paste them only as labelled examples.

Change the scene only when testing context transfer: the relationship and unknown position stay the same, with numbers nearby. A new scene must change the everyday activity, objects, and actions; changing only names of people or objects is still the same scene. Success only in the original scene is recorded as evidence of relationship or number variation in that scene; only if the student can still explain after the scene changes is it recorded as context transfer.

Adjust story complexity by the student's performance, not tied to grade: a one-step story with complete information first, then one extra condition or intermediate step at a time. In grades 5–6 a two- or three-step story, a ratio, or a percent situation can be the target itself when the evidence supports it.

When the student authors the problem, its conditions are theirs to change: reuse a structure from `references/mathematical-strategies.md` only when the student chose to keep it, and have the answerer's check `references/math-student-problem-design.md` owns decide whether changing a condition when the student drafts it is a silent rewrite or the student's own decision.

For fixed-total contribution differences, organized finite choices, or repeating states/positions, use `references/mathematical-strategies.md` to design cases from structure rather than a type-name/strategy match. Distinguish near transfer (new surface, same structure) from boundary discrimination (change one decisive condition and examine whether the method still applies). Examples include fixed versus variable unit adjustment, ordered roles versus an unranked pair, or a stated repeat rule versus a finite prefix. Reuse valid student alternatives; no method name is required. Transfer is optional and needs agreement; without independent evidence keep transfer unknown, not inferred from repeating the teacher's summary.

When the contrast changes one load-bearing condition rather than the surface, read `references/boundary-variation.md` for the one-condition change, the three outcomes and the survival statement. This file still designs the original and the neighbouring task.

## Multi-Part Exercises

When the student brings a printed exercise with several sub-questions on one quantity relationship, or when the chosen problem naturally has several graded parts, design the parts as a task bundle: a child view with one entry part, then one variation part, then one check part, and an adult key that stays hidden until the student has attempted, asked, or graded. The student does the organisation of a multi-part answer; the teaching assistant chooses at most one entry part this turn and does not list all parts as pending questions.

## Task Verification

Before presenting a new problem, verify the conditions, the arithmetic, the units, and uniqueness the same way. In the problem-maker act the student's submitted problem is checked before answering it: a scripted solution reaching only intended values does not make the student's conditions coherent, so an inconsistency between the student's numbers stays in the clarification rather than in a corrected problem. Open problems need success criteria, not a simulated unique answer; an invented real-world figure must not be presented as factual data.
