# Derivation Teaching

Use with `references/math-teaching-core.md` and `references/scaffold-calibration.md` for discovering or understanding a formula or general rule, or deriving a solution through linked quantity relationships in a multi-step problem. For multi-condition or multi-stage word problems, also read `references/word-problem-derivation.md`; it specializes the same loop for intermediate quantities, compatible intervals, and task validation. This reference owns the planning-and-bridging loop, not a catalogue of proofs. For constructing, understanding, checking, repairing, or organizing a proof, also read `references/proof-teaching.md`; it adds claim analysis, strategic choices, validity checks, and proof-specific evidence without replacing this loop. **Backward planning is for the teacher; forward reasoning is for the student.** Use CGI's attention to the student's actual strategies to adapt the bridge, not to stage a predetermined discovery. In Feynman mode, keep the student as presenter.

## 1. Establish the destination

Keep the actual expression, claim, scope, and requested depth from the conversation. Resolve a name from context; ask one short clarification only when different meanings would change the task.

**Output boundary:** a conjecture is a candidate pattern; a derivation establishes a relationship from stated assumptions; a proof justifies the claim over its entire scope. A derivation can also be a proof when its steps and scope warrant it. Finite examples can suggest a universal claim but cannot establish it without a general reason.

For a concrete word problem, the destination may be a quantity and a justified solution rather than a new formula or universal claim. Start a purposeful bridge without a discovery menu. Apply **Guide before revealing** and its **Informed full-solution choice** exception in `learn-core`'s `references/pedagogy-core.md` to every target solution. The Formula-goal gate below adds goal selection for formula/general-method learning, not every use of a known relationship.

**Formula-goal gate:** establish the mathematical learning goal before choosing a bridge. Discovering a route, understanding an already-supplied rule or proof, and applying a formula are different goals, all subject to the shared reveal rules. Agreed new learning can use the shared separate-example entry without recasting it as independent discovery. For understanding a supplied rule, connect its terms and rationale. Keep discovery's target rule and decisive inference private; reference lookup and complete-target requests retain their distinct paths below.

- An explicit agreed “teach me this new method; I haven't learned it” may enter session's `micro_lesson` through the shared contract loaded via `math-common`, with no compulsory discovery menu. Teach understanding or application of a supplied rule as that goal, not independent derivation. An agreed self-derivation still protects its target formula and decisive inference; “teach” is not a keyword override. Reference lookup and explicit complete-target requests keep their paths below.
- For a new formula topic or a request for a faster general method, withhold the target formula while the intended depth is unresolved. Use a goal-relevant exploration, or ask one short clarification when the choice changes the next action, following shared question/choice rules.
- Treat a confirmation of expression or scope as only that: it does not authorize formula delivery. Wanting less repetitive calculation establishes an obstacle, not inability to reason. If a teacher question mixed scope and permission to reveal, separate them. Clear assent to an explicit full-solution offer follows the shared informed-choice path, not an inference of inability.
- If the student wants to derive the rule or asks how one could think of it, proceed from their tools through steps 2–6. Give the smallest relevant hint or explanation of a choice, preserving a meaningful inference. An ambiguous request to explain or demonstrate concerns the current obstacle, not permission to complete the route.
- If the student explicitly requests the target formula or a complete worked application, explanation, or proof for the learning task, apply the shared **Informed full-solution choice**. Ordinary “explain this” requests remain guided. An existing or student-supplied formula may be used for the agreed application task without reopening discovery, while the student carries its application. Ordinary reference lookup is distinct from solving or deriving a target; a reference-only reply may end without a mandatory exercise or follow-up question. A concrete tool gap enters a recorded tool-learning child per `references/prerequisite-learning.md`, under the same shared reveal rules.
- If the formula was already revealed prematurely, acknowledge the mismatch, retire the unwanted application question, and resume the requested reasoning goal. Subsequent reconstruction is not independent discovery merely because the student completes later algebra.

Completion: the next action matches the goal and shared reveal rules; scope confirmation or method choice alone has not authorized a full solution. Once discovery or derivation is agreed, keep it in view rather than quietly substituting formula verification.

## 2. Locate the student starting point

Start from actions and relationships the student has actually shown, including their own representation and partial solution. Separate relevant tools as **demonstrated / self-reported / unknown**, retaining support conditions. A self-report is provisionally available, unconfirmed; try it in the task without awarding independent mastery. Grade and unfamiliar terminology do not establish a tool gap. Reuse earlier evidence rather than retesting merely because a session is new.

A statement of no ideas does not locate the obstacle by itself. If the student later says they can perform the basic calculation but want a general method, retain that ability as self-reported, provisionally use it, and return to the generalization problem rather than restarting elementary calculation checks. A correction of the teaching goal is not evidence of a prerequisite gap.

A **prerequisite tool** is an action: pairing ends, expanding a product, regrouping a count, comparing changes, or cancelling consecutive terms. Distinguish **Execution:** performing it, from **Selection:** recognising when and why it helps. Record the current obstacle in terms of the student's work, not a list of missing curriculum topics.

Completion: there is an evidenced starting relationship and a small set of route-relevant unknowns, not an exhaustive knowledge inventory. With no evidence yet, the opening task in step 4 locates the starting point.

## 3. Plan backward across candidate routes

Privately solve and verify two or three plausible routes when available. From each conclusion, work backward through the necessary relationships to the student's starting point. A finished proof establishes mathematical validity; it does not establish that its steps are discoverable by this student. For each route identify:

- The prerequisite tools and critical choices, including any teacher-supplied construction.
- The student-visible clue that could motivate each critical choice, its expected benefit, and how the student could check it.
- Where demonstrated tools connect, the nearest uncertain bridge, and what response would support continuing or revising it.

Prefer the fewest unsupported choices and a reachable next bridge, not just the shortest proof or smallest arithmetic. Consider the load of new representations and tools, and whether intermediate work produces understandable progress. Keep one route provisional and useful alternatives available; do not show the student the whole route tree or its answers.

**Justify the exact choice.** Motivate a particular multiplier, factorization, or auxiliary object from available clues without relying on the unknown conclusion. Clearing a denominator already present is different from choosing a constant because it occurs in the teacher's target answer. With no source for its value, keep the multiplier or construction open and seek a relation that could determine it, or choose another bridge. Apply the candidate-source check in step 4: an expected benefit alone does not explain how to generate a candidate. A heuristic trial need not be logically inevitable; a genuinely new construction belongs to explicit teaching under the shared reveal gate, not renamed student discovery.

Completion: at least one verified route has a plausible connection to the starting point, a next bridge, and an observable reason to continue or change it. If none is currently reachable, choose one purposeful tool-building task rather than forcing a hidden-answer sequence.

## 4. 先搭桥，再推导 — choose the student-owned decision

For a task requiring conception, first help the student connect **what I can already do → what could help with this unknown**, then derive. Before choosing the opening action, identify the **one consequential route decision** most worth leaving to this student: a representation, conjecture, transformation, intermediate quantity, auxiliary object, or criterion for a route to work. This is not necessarily the first calculation. Keep it open in both the question and any teacher figure. Routine fluency work needs no added planning stage; understanding a supplied method retains that goal rather than pretending its route is undiscovered.

| Current evidence | First useful action |
|---|---|
| No overall image of the relationships | Invite one purposeful representation or action: draw, point, make a table, try a case, or state the key relationship. Establish what must be represented or preserved before asking for a formula. |
| Relevant tools are usable but not connected to the target | Put one evidenced tool and the actual unknown within view; leave one substantive choice of how to connect them. Supply only the direction needed, not method plus construction plus steps. |
| A necessary tool is confirmed missing | Use the recorded tool-learning child below; teach only the dependency needed for the current route, then return. “No ideas” alone leaves the cause unknown. |
| The student already offers a viable route | Skip a route-finding probe. Continue that route; check a necessary condition only if unresolved. An already-shown connection needs no repeated “how did you think of that?” report. |
| An attempt exists but its value is unclear | Identify what it established and what remains to reach the target; leave a grounded continue, revise, or switch decision where useful. A stall alone warrants neither instant replacement nor endless persistence. |

A drawing, pointing gesture, trial, or equation can **show the connection** without a verbal plan. Forming a representation or connection is mathematical work; judging why it helps and when to continue or switch is monitoring. Elicit only the evidence needed at this decision, not a complete plan or a fixed introspection interview. A supplied direction is supported conception, even when the student makes the remaining choice.

Completion: one consequential decision remains student-owned and the opening matches their evidenced starting point; or an already-proposed viable route goes directly to derivation. Only a decision-changing unknown warrants a probe.

### Step back: strategy → candidate → execution

When the next move has no accessible source, or the student asks why one would think of it, **step back** from the particular expression to a reusable operation and its applicability. A **strategy** names a way to reduce the obstacle (for example, organise cases to conjecture a relationship, or rewrite a sum so interior terms cancel). A **candidate** is a particular expression, arrangement, or auxiliary object that might realise it. **Execution** expands, calculates, or checks that candidate. Giving a candidate and asking for execution bypasses strategy selection and candidate generation, even if the calculation is easy.

Anchor the bridge in one understood prior action or a visible relationship in the current task. Formula recall alone does not establish that the student knows the operation behind it. Help extract what was changed, what became simpler, and what condition made it work; use that to frame one reachable strategy or candidate decision. A general label alone (such as “find a pattern”) is insufficient: leave a concrete action that could produce or test a relationship, keeping finite evidence distinct from proof. Offer only as much direction as needed; a fixed strategy menu or a compulsory abstraction interview adds load rather than a bridge. Follow a viable student route directly.

**Clue-first pause:** when the student has correctly interpreted a trial, briefly connect that observed result to the goal, then ask an open next-move question and wait. Keep the choice of what to change with the student, including the search dimension (such as exponent, operation, representation, or auxiliary object). A concrete observed clue plus a clear desired effect is enough to ground this question; the next action need not already be named. For example, after the student obtains `2k+1` from `(k+1)²−k²`, invite them to consider what they could do to leave a square term, rather than asking which power of adjacent numbers to subtract. The latter supplies both the object family and the operation even without saying “cube”. If the student then asks for help or shows a specific obstacle, narrow one dimension with the smallest useful hint under the shared guidance rules. A correct short response alone is not a reason to add that hint. This pause is for student-owned route finding; an already chosen route, agreed instruction, or authorized full solution keeps its existing branch.

**Candidate-source check (teacher-private):** without knowing the target answer, would the available clue explain which family of objects is worth examining and how to form or screen a candidate? Use the student's prior operation, the current structure, or an actual trial as the source. “This would produce the term we want” names a desired outcome, not by itself a way to find that particular construction. When the source is missing, keep the candidate open and narrow one decision; if the construction is genuinely new knowledge, offer it only through the existing permitted demonstration paths and label the teacher contribution. The label itself does not authorize disclosure of a protected task's decisive move.

Treat a heuristic as a test, not an inevitable deduction: identify what result would make it useful, then use what happened to retain, revise, or reject it. A failed candidate may reveal a feature needed in the next one; it does not mandate a predetermined next formula. When a separate example is appropriate, extract its reusable operation and applicability, rather than merely changing the numbers in the same concealed construction. Reduce working-memory demand by narrowing one selection decision or externalising a relationship, not by supplying the entire route as many arithmetic blanks. Keep only the next necessary tool or representation in play; unknown execution readiness is not a diagnosed gap.

Completion: there is an accessible source and a testable purpose for the next trial, with one meaningful generation, screening, or monitoring decision left to the student when route finding is the goal; or a permitted teacher-supplied construction is honestly being understood, not claimed as discovered. These are conditional repairs, not extra stages for routine application, reference lookup, or an authorized full solution. Retain only observable choices and supplied support in the existing evidence fields, not the teacher-private candidate search.

### Probe only the uncertain connection

Use the CGI-informed loop **pose → observe → interpret → bridge**. Ask a minimal task only when its response would change the next teaching decision; otherwise continue from existing evidence. Prefer work within the actual problem. Use a short variant probe only when it isolates a route-critical uncertainty, then return as soon as the link is usable. Keep preliminary probes few and purposeful; further clarification is warranted only when its answer would materially change the route or support. Prefer evidence from doing the derivation to an expanding entrance test; reuse answers already given.

A probe leaves something real to the student: proposing a representation, trying a transformation, explaining a comparison, or judging whether an attempt helps. Its numbers and conditions should make an existing strategy usable while revealing the needed relationship. Observe the strategy, not only the answer. Asking whether a method is familiar or rehearsing unrelated arithmetic does not locate the bridge.

**Next-step readiness:** use an evidenced tool or give room for an uncertain but accessible attempt. Unknown is not the same as missing. For a confirmed missing tool, follow `references/prerequisite-learning.md`: preserve this task, enter the nearest tool-learning child through session dispatch, and recursively resolve its evidenced dependencies. Inside a ready child, one explicitly introduced tool may be learned with a reasoned example followed by a student attempt and fresh independent use; do not stack several unfamiliar tools into a comparison question or call a parent-level demonstration a repaired gap.

Completion: the response supplies evidence for continuing, teaching a specific tool, or trying an alternative. If the task is unanswered, wait; do not invent the evidence or proceed down the route.

## 5. Teach forward with visible progress

At the next meaningful decision, connect **obstacle → useful structure → candidate move** using the student's own reasoning. Make clear what is already known, what is still difficult, and what this attempt could settle. Offer bounded direction while leaving a mathematical choice or inference to the student. A supplied starting relation may help; the central reasoning being elicited remains theirs.

**Selection is not execution.** An easy calculation may conceal a large choice. Explain the purpose before assigning it; routine arithmetic alone does not show the student can select the method. Distinguish deduction from an exploratory **Heuristic trial** and a teacher's **Supplied construction**. Explain the clue and expected use of a trial; explain and label a supplied construction, then let the student investigate its consequence. Calling it a suggestion is not its rationale.

Give concrete feedback on what the attempt established: a useful relationship, an obstacle removed, or a candidate ruled out. An unsuccessful trial can constrain the next search without being called a success. Visible progress means the student can see why the work was worthwhile, not promised easy steps, guaranteed insight, or praise without mathematical content.

Pace by decisions, not lines: keep one coherent thinking task per turn and one pending question. Combine familiar mechanical manipulations when they serve that decision; avoid breaking a productive attempt at every algebra line. For the smallest genuinely missing tool, use the recorded child-task entry and single-parent return in `references/prerequisite-learning.md`, then reconnect it to the original obstacle. Do not complete all the strategic work and leave only a pattern-label or arithmetic blank.

Completion: one attempt, inference, or tool-building step is ready for the student's response, with an intelligible purpose and a meaningful action still theirs.

## 6. Update the route from the response

Use actual new evidence before selecting the next bridge:

- If the strategy advances the goal, build on it and fade support. Follow a valid student alternative even when it differs from the teacher's preferred proof; check its mathematics and replan from there.
- If execution blocks progress, locate the specific tool gap and supply the smallest useful support. If tools are available but the purpose is unclear, repair the selection bridge instead of repeating calculations.
- If the student asks why a move was chosen or sees no benefit, pause the calculation and return to the selection point. Check for a valid equivalent operation before correcting their description. When the rationale was unsupported, acknowledge it and withdraw the unsupported move; replan from the last established relationship.
- If a trial fails, use what it rules out to select the next one. Two unproductive prompts call for reviewing the bridge, prerequisite, or route; they do not establish weak calculation or justify a default downgrade.

Keep a pattern-based conjecture provisional in speech and records until a reason covers its scope. Recognising a formula in teacher-selected data is supported pattern recognition; routine rearrangement does not turn it into proof. Follow lifecycle for ending or topic change. Switch to verify-and-use only by agreement, stating that no derivation was done if that is the case.

Completion: the next action follows the student's evidence, not the desire to finish the original script. Update only the necessary continuation state; an unanswered question still waits.

## 7. Completion and recording

Judge against the agreed goal. For learning to find a route, distinguish supported conception from an unhinted choice on a fresh task. When a transfer check is useful and agreed, omit method cues; a changed condition that makes the old method inconvenient can reveal a grounded new direction or a justified switch. A defensible student-found route is progress even if later arithmetic is wrong; record each separately. One successful transfer attempt is not durable mastery, and essential proof inferences remain required.

For a multi-step word problem, use `references/word-problem-derivation.md` to check student-owned relationship selection, connection to the requested quantity, and validation against the original conditions; a numerical answer alone does not establish route selection. For discovery-oriented learning, look for why a route was worth trying, a student-carried key inference, and recognition of when the tool may help on a nearby task. For understanding a supplied proof, look for justified connections through its key step. For independent proof construction, use the evidence criteria in `references/proof-teaching.md`; guided completion is not independent construction. Finite examples and proof recitation alone do not establish independent route discovery.

No new profile field is added. In existing `context.turn_state`, retain the provisional route, relevant tools with evidence/support conditions, the current bridge or obstacle, supplied help, and pending question. Keep alternative routes only when needed for continuation; do not store a full private deliberation. In the segment distinguish student strategy, teacher proposals, supported work, and what remains unknown. An unfinished proof is not by itself a student difficulty. Durable skill confirmation follows the shared evidence thresholds.
